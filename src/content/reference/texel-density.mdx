Texel density is how many texture pixels cover a given amount of world space.
That is all it is. It is also the single most common source of a kit looking
subtly amateur, because inconsistent density is visible exactly where two
surfaces meet — and it is very cheap to get right if you decide the number once,
up front.

## The formula

```text
density (px/cm) = texture resolution / surface size in cm
density (px/m)  = density (px/cm) × 100
```

A 2048 map on a 200&nbsp;cm surface gives 10.24&nbsp;px/cm, which is
1024&nbsp;px/m. Industry convention states the per-metre figure, so that surface
is "1K per metre".

## Standard targets

<div class="table-scroll">

| Target | px/m | px/cm | Use for |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Background | 256 | 2.56 | Distant architecture, terrain, anything the camera never approaches |
| **Common standard** | **512** | **5.12** | Most game environments. The default answer if you have no reason to pick another |
| Close-up | 1024 | 10.24 | Hero props, interactive objects, anything within a metre of the camera |
| Cinematic | 2048 | 20.48 | First-person hands, weapons, cinematic close-ups |
| Extreme | 4096 | 40.96 | Rarely justified. Usually a sign the surface should be split |

</div>

<Callout kind="key">
Consistency beats the specific value. A whole kit at 512 reads as professional.
A kit that averages 512 but mixes 1024 and 256 reads as careless, because your
eye catches the mismatch at the seam between two pieces even when neither piece
is wrong on its own.
</Callout>

## Quick conversions

The table you actually keep coming back for: what surface size a given texture
covers at each standard density.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Texture | @ 256 px/m | @ 512 px/m | @ 1024 px/m | @ 2048 px/m |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 256 | 100 cm | 50 cm | 25 cm | 12.5 cm |
| 512 | 200 cm | 100 cm | 50 cm | 25 cm |
| 1024 | 400 cm | 200 cm | 100 cm | 50 cm |
| 2048 | 800 cm | 400 cm | 200 cm | 100 cm |
| 4096 | 1600 cm | 800 cm | 400 cm | 200 cm |
| 8192 | 3200 cm | 1600 cm | 800 cm | 400 cm |

</div>

Read it as: at the common 512&nbsp;px/m standard, a 2K map covers a 4-metre
surface. That one fact answers most day-to-day questions.

## Checking it in Unreal

The engine will tell you rather than making you measure. In the viewport, switch
to `Optimization Viewmodes → Texture Density`. Green is roughly on target, red is
over-textured, blue is under.

For a numeric answer on a specific mesh, the checker-texture method is still the
most reliable: apply a material with a checker pattern at a known scale and count
squares. Unreal ships one at
`/Engine/EngineMaterials/DefaultDiffuse` — but a purpose-made checker with
labelled 10&nbsp;cm squares is worth making once and keeping in your starter
project forever.

<Callout kind="warn">
The Texture Density viewmode measures the *material's* density, which is not the
same as the density of the individual texture if the material scales UVs. If a
material multiplies UVs by 4, the viewmode reports the effective density
correctly and your source texture is four times coarser than you might assume
from the map resolution alone. Check both.
</Callout>

## When to deliberately break the rule

There are three legitimate reasons, and "this asset felt important" is not one
of them.

1. **Camera distance genuinely differs.** A skybox building at 256 and a door
   handle at 1024 in the same scene is correct, not inconsistent, because nothing
   ever sees both at the same apparent size.
2. **Trim sheets concentrate detail.** A trim sheet legitimately runs higher
   density than the large flat surfaces it decorates, because the trim carries the
   detail and the flats carry the area.
3. **Tiling rate compensates.** A material that tiles 8× across a wall gets its
   effective density from the tiling rate, not the map size. Judge the result on
   screen, not the number in the asset browser.
