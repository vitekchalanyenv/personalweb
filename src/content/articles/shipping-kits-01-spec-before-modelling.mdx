I have shipped three commercial modular environment kits. Two of them went
smoothly. One of them took roughly three weeks longer than it should have, and
I can point at the exact reason: I started modelling before I could answer the
question *"how many times does this piece appear in a scene?"*

That question sounds trivial. It is not. It determines the polycount you can
afford, whether the piece gets a unique texture or shares a trim sheet, whether
it snaps to a grid, and — the expensive one — whether it needs to work at three
different scales or one. Answer it wrong on a 116-mesh kit and you find out at
the point where fixing it means redoing UVs on forty assets.

So now I write a spec first. It is one page. It takes an afternoon. It is the
single highest-leverage hour in the whole project.

<Callout kind="key">
A kit is not a collection of props. It is a **system with a contract**: pieces
that fit each other, at stated dimensions, with a stated texture budget. The
spec is where you write that contract down — before you can still change it
cheaply.
</Callout>

## Why "just start blocking out" fails specifically for kits

Blocking out and iterating is genuinely good advice for a *scene*. A scene has
one camera, one composition, one lighting setup, and you find the answers by
looking. A kit has none of those things. It has an unknown buyer building an
unknown scene, and your job is to make every reasonable arrangement work.

The failure mode is subtle. Everything looks fine while you are building the
first fifteen pieces, because you are unconsciously arranging them the way you
imagined them. The trouble arrives when you try the twentieth arrangement — the
one your buyer will try — and discover that your wall pieces only line up if
the corner goes on the left.

This is the part where I should say what actually happened to me.

<Callout kind="wrong">
On the cabin kit I authored the wall segments first, at what felt like a
natural length, and derived the corner pieces from them afterwards. The corners
ended up needing a 6&nbsp;cm overlap to hide a seam. Which was fine — until I
built an L-shaped floor plan and the overlap put the roof beam 6&nbsp;cm off the
wall it was supposed to sit on. Every roof piece inherited the error. I refit
eleven meshes.

The fix was not a modelling fix. It was that I never decided, in writing,
whether the corner or the wall owned the grid.
</Callout>

## The spec, in four decisions

Not a template to fill in mechanically — four questions that each rule out a
lot of downstream work.

### 1. What is the module length, and what owns the grid?

Pick one base length and derive everything from it. I use **200&nbsp;cm** for
building kits, because it divides cleanly into a 100&nbsp;cm sub-module and sits
comfortably against Unreal's default 100&nbsp;cm grid, so a buyer can snap
pieces without changing their editor settings.

Then decide, explicitly, **which piece owns the origin**. My rule now: corners
own the grid, walls fill between them. Corners are the pieces with two
constraints, so they get to be right and the walls absorb the tolerance.

That last row is worth stating in your store listing, not just your spec. "Snaps
on the default grid" removes a real objection for a buyer who has been burned
by a kit built on 137&nbsp;cm modules.

### 2. How many times does each piece appear?

Sort every planned asset into three buckets. This one table decides your whole
texture budget.

<div class="table-scroll">

| Frequency | Appears | Texture strategy | Polycount |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | 20+ times per scene | Shared trim sheet, no unique maps | Tightest — this is where triangles multiply |
| **Character** | 3–8 times | Trim sheet plus a small unique atlas | Moderate |
| **Hero** | Once, maybe twice | Unique texture set, worth the memory | Generous |

</div>

The mistake — and it is the most common mistake in kits generally — is treating
every prop as a hero prop. If a piece appears twenty times, a unique 2K map for
it is twenty times the memory for one instance of visual interest. Put that
budget into the one piece the camera actually stops on.

### 3. What goes on the trim sheet, and what does not?

Once you know the frequency buckets, the trim sheet almost writes itself: every
structural piece has to live on it. The decision that remains is *how many
trims*, and the honest answer is fewer than you want.

My working rule is **one trim sheet per material family**, not per asset type.
The cabin kit has two: painted timber, and stone-and-mortar. Metal fittings share
the timber sheet because there are only four of them and they are small.

A trim sheet plan is a drawing, not a list. Draw the sheet at the start, with
the strips labelled and the UV space allocated, before you author a single
texel. If the strips do not fit, you have too many, and finding that out on
paper costs ten minutes instead of two days.

### 4. What is the texel density, and is it the same for everything?

One number, written down, applied to the whole set. I use **512&nbsp;px/m** for
kits like this, which is the common game standard and holds up until the camera
is closer than about a metre.

Consistency matters more than the value. A kit where every piece is 512 reads as
professional; a kit where some pieces are 1024 and some are 256 reads as
careless even if the average is fine, because the mismatch is visible exactly
where two pieces meet.

If you are not sure what number your surface sizes imply, the
[texel density reference page](/reference/texel-density/) has a calculator that
will tell you in about four seconds.

## What the spec is not

It is not a design document and it should not be pretty. Mine is a single page
of plain text and one hand-drawn trim sheet layout. Nobody else reads it. Its
whole job is to force the four decisions above into an explicit form so that
when I am eleven assets deep and tempted to fudge something, there is a written
answer to argue with.

It is also **not immutable**. Change it when you learn something — but change
the document, not just the model. The document being stale is how you end up
with two conventions in one kit.

<Callout kind="warn">
The version to watch out for is a spec written *after* you started modelling, to
describe what you already did. That is documentation, which is useful, but it is
not a spec and it will not save you three weeks. The value is entirely in the
decisions being made while they are still cheap.
</Callout>

## The one-page template

Here is the actual shape of mine, stripped to headings. Copy it, delete what
does not apply, and fill it in before you open anything.

```text
KIT: <name>
TARGET: <engine version, renderer features you rely on>
THEME: <one sentence — what a buyer would search for>

GRID
  base module          <cm>
  sub-modules          <cm>
  vertical module      <cm>
  grid owner           <corners | walls>
  pivot convention     <...>
  snaps on default grid?  <yes/no>

TEXEL DENSITY
  target               <px/m>
  exceptions           <which pieces, and why>

TRIM SHEETS
  sheet 1              <material family>  <resolution>
  sheet 2              <material family>  <resolution>
  drawn layout         <link to the sketch>

ASSET LIST
  structural (20+ uses)    <count>   trim only
  character  (3-8 uses)    <count>   trim + small atlas
  hero       (1-2 uses)    <count>   unique set

BUDGETS
  triangle ceiling, structural piece   <n>
  LOD count                            <n>
  collision                            <simple / per-piece / hull>
  total texture memory target           <MB>

DELIVERABLE
  formats              <UE, FBX, glTF...>
  demo level?          <yes/no>
  documentation        <what the buyer gets>

OPEN QUESTIONS
  <the things you genuinely do not know yet — keep this list honest>
```

That `OPEN QUESTIONS` section is the most useful part. Everything you are
tempted to hand-wave goes there, visibly, and you resolve it before it becomes
a refit.
