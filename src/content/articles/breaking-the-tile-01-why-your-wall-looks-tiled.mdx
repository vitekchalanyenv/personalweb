Everybody knows a tiled texture when they see one. Very few people can say
*what* they are seeing, which is why the usual response — go get a bigger
texture — does not work and costs memory to fail.

Here is the useful framing. Your eye is not detecting the texture. It is
detecting a **regular spatial frequency**: some feature repeating at an exact
interval. A bigger texture moves that interval further apart. It does not remove
it. Walk backwards and the repetition comes right back, now at a scale that
covers half your screen.

So the whole discipline of breaking tiling is: **add variation at frequencies
your tile does not contain**, especially frequencies much lower than the tile
itself.

<Callout kind="key">
Repetition reads as repetition when the eye can find the period. Fixing it means
adding information at a *different* period — usually much larger than one tile —
not adding more information inside the tile.
</Callout>

## Name what you are seeing first

Before touching a graph, work out which of these you have. They have different
fixes and people routinely apply the wrong one.

<div class="table-scroll">

| What you see | The actual cause | What fixes it |
|---|---|---|
| **A visible grid** | Value differences at the tile edge | Macro variation (this article) |
| **The same blemish everywhere** | A high-contrast unique feature inside the tile | Remove it from the tile; add it back as a decal |
| **Regular lighting bands** | Normal or roughness repeating, not albedo | Break roughness separately from albedo |
| **Obvious seams at UV edges** | The texture is not actually tileable | Fix the source texture — no shader saves this |
| **Repetition only at distance** | Mip levels converging to a flat average | Distance-based blend, later in this series |

</div>

The first row is the one you have about eighty per cent of the time, and it is
the one with the cheapest fix by a wide margin.

## The four-node fix

Macro variation. One extra texture, sampled at a much larger scale than the base,
multiplied over the top, and then blended back so it does not turn to mud.

The number that matters is that second UV scale. If your base tiles at 8× across
a surface, the macro noise wants to be somewhere around **0.25×** — roughly one
thirtieth of the base frequency. Get it wrong in the obvious direction and you
have just added a second visible tile.

### The parameter values I actually ship

<div class="table-scroll">

| Parameter | My default | Range worth trying |
|---|---|---|
| Macro UV scale | `0.03` of base tiling | `0.015`–`0.06` |
| Blend strength | `0.35` | `0.2`–`0.5` |
| Noise contrast | Soft — no hard edges | — |
| Applied to | Albedo, and roughness at half strength | — |

</div>

That last row is the one people skip and it is the difference between "less
tiled" and "not tiled". If you only break the albedo, the specular response
still repeats at the original period, and in any scene with a moving light your
eye locks straight onto it. Feed the same noise into roughness at about half the
strength.

<Callout kind="wrong">
My first version of this drove the noise into the normal map too. Do not. It
looks fine in the material preview and then reads as a lumpy, inflated surface
in the scene, because you are effectively adding low-frequency geometry that the
lighting has to justify and the silhouette contradicts. Albedo and roughness
only.
</Callout>

## What it costs

The reason to reach for this first is not that it is the most powerful technique
in the series. It is that it is nearly free, so it is the correct default on
every tiling material you author.

Nine instructions and one shared 512 map. That is the whole bill.

<Callout kind="warn">
Share the noise texture across your entire material library. One 512 greyscale
referenced by forty materials costs 0.7&nbsp;MB once. Forty bespoke noise maps
cost forty times that and look no better, and this is a genuinely common way for
a kit's memory budget to quietly double.
</Callout>

## When this is not the answer

Macro variation fixes value repetition. It does not fix:

- **Shape repetition.** If your texture contains one distinctive crack and that
  crack appears every two metres, no amount of tonal variation hides it. Take the
  feature out of the tile and reintroduce it as a decal where you want it.
- **Genuine seams.** If the source texture does not tile, fix the source
  texture. A shader cannot invent the pixels that would have matched.
- **Distance convergence.** Once mips flatten the surface to an average colour,
  macro variation flattens with it. That needs a distance-based blend, which is
  part four.
