If you search for how to get SpeedTree vegetation into Unreal 5 as Nanite
foliage, you will find a handful of paid courses, two or three YouTube videos,
and a pirated copy of one of the courses. What you will not find is a free,
current, written account of the whole chain with numbers attached.

That is a strange gap, because this is not a niche problem — it is the core
technical question for anyone doing natural environments, and the answer changed
substantially with Nanite foliage support. So this is that account. It is written
against **UE 5.7** and **SpeedTree for Games 9**, and every number in it was
measured on the same forest scene.

## The decision that dominates everything else

Before any export settings: **masked or opaque?**

Traditional game foliage uses alpha-masked cards — a quad with a leaf texture and
an opacity mask. It is cheap in triangles and it has been the standard for twenty
years. Nanite changes the arithmetic, because Nanite does not love masked
materials. Masked geometry forces a separate pass, defeats some of Nanite's
rasterisation advantages, and interacts badly with Lumen, where every masked
surface is additional work for the ray tracer.

The alternative is **modelled leaves**: actual geometry per leaf cluster, opaque
material, no opacity mask at all. Twenty years ago that was absurd. With Nanite
it is often *faster*, because Nanite's whole premise is that triangle count
stops being the thing you budget.

<Callout kind="key">
The counterintuitive result: for Nanite foliage, **more triangles and no alpha
mask frequently beats fewer triangles with a mask.** Nanite is good at geometry
and bad at masks. Build to its strengths rather than porting a 2005 pipeline
into it.
</Callout>

Here is what that swap actually did on the test scene.

The cost is real: disk size roughly doubles, and the SpeedTree work is more
involved because you are modelling leaf clusters rather than mapping cards. But
frame time is what ships a scene, and the shadow and Lumen savings compound with
every additional light.

<Callout kind="warn">
This result is scene-dependent and I want to be honest about the boundary. It
holds for **dense, close-range vegetation with dynamic lighting**. For distant
background trees, sparse scatter, or a baked-lighting project, masked cards can
still win. Measure your own scene — do not take my forest as universal.
</Callout>

## The export chain, step by step

### 1. In SpeedTree

<div class="table-scroll">

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Leaf geometry | Modelled clusters, not single cards | The point of the whole exercise |
| Mesh LODs | Generate 1 low LOD only | Nanite handles the rest. You need one cheap LOD as a fallback and for the billboard transition |
| Wind | Enabled, exported to vertex colours | See the wind section below |
| Vertex colours | **Do not overwrite** | UE reads wind data from these channels — this is the single most common way to break wind on import |
| Scale | Centimetres | Match Unreal or you will rescale 18 assets by hand |
| Pivot | Base of trunk, centred | So foliage scatter and physics behave |

</div>

### 2. On import into Unreal

Enable Nanite on the trunk and branch meshes immediately. For the leaf clusters,
enable Nanite **and** set the material to `Opaque` — if it is still `Masked` you
have not actually made the swap and you will get the worst of both approaches.

Then the setting almost everyone misses:

<Callout kind="warn">
**Preserve Area** on the Nanite settings. Off by default. With it off, Nanite's
simplification collapses thin leaf geometry into slivers at distance and your
canopy visibly thins out as the camera pulls back. Turn it on for anything with
thin features. It costs a little in cluster efficiency and saves the entire
silhouette of your tree.
</Callout>

### 3. Wind that does not look like jelly

Wind is where good foliage becomes convincing and where most foliage becomes
faintly ridiculous. The problem is almost always that everything moves *together*
— one global sine wave, and the forest breathes in unison like a single organism.

The fix is per-instance phase offset. Three layers, each at a different frequency:

<NodeGraph
  src="/img/grown-not-placed-01/wind-graph.webp"
  alt="Unreal material graph layering three wind frequencies with a per-instance random phase offset"
  caption="Three-layer wind with per-instance phase. The PerInstanceRandom node is the entire difference between a forest and a jelly."
  engine="Unreal Engine 5.7"
  nodes={[
    ['PerInstanceRandom', 'a stable 0–1 value per foliage instance — this is what desynchronises the forest'],
    ['Multiply', 'scale that random into a phase offset in radians'],
    ['Time', 'scaled three times, at roughly 0.4, 1.6 and 5.0 — trunk sway, branch bend, leaf flutter'],
    ['Sine', 'one per frequency layer, each with the per-instance phase added'],
    ['Add', 'sum the three layers, weighted heavily toward the slowest'],
    ['VertexColor', 'the SpeedTree wind weights — R for trunk, G for branch, B for leaf — masking each layer to the right part of the tree'],
    ['WorldPositionOffset', 'the final displacement'],
  ]}
/>

Weight the layers **heavily toward the slowest**. My defaults are roughly
`0.6 / 0.3 / 0.1` for trunk, branch and leaf. Trees are mostly still; it is the
occasional slow lean that reads as wind, not constant fluttering.

## Where the frames actually go

The single most useful thing I can give you is not a settings list, it is the
order in which to look for problems. From the same test scene, ranked by how
much a bad decision costs you:

<div class="table-scroll">

| Cost | Usually caused by | First thing to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Virtual shadow maps** | Every foliage instance casting a full dynamic shadow | Disable shadow casting on small ground foliage. It is almost never visible and it is expensive |
| **Lumen scene lighting** | Masked materials, and too much foliage in the Lumen scene | The masked-to-opaque swap; then `r.Lumen.Scene.SurfaceCache` resolution |
| **Overdraw** | Layered translucent or masked cards | `Shader Complexity` view mode. Bright red canopies are the culprit |
| **Base pass** | Genuinely too many unique materials | Consolidate to one master material with instances |
| **Triangle count** | Rarely the actual problem with Nanite | Check this last, not first |

</div>

That ordering is the opposite of most people's instinct. Triangle count is the
thing artists worry about and, with Nanite, the thing that matters least. Shadow
casting on grass is the thing nobody thinks about and it can cost several
milliseconds.

## What I still have not solved

Two honest gaps, because a guide that claims to have everything solved is
usually lying:

- **Seasonal variation from one asset.** I have a working mask-and-LUT approach
  that handles green to autumn convincingly. Winter — bare branches with snow
  load — genuinely needs different geometry, and I have not found a way around
  that which does not look wrong. That is a later part in this series, when I
  have something worth publishing rather than a workaround.
- **Billboard transition at extreme distance.** The pop is reduced but not gone.
  It is currently the weakest part of my forest and I would rather say so than
  pretend the cull distance solves it.
